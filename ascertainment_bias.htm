The More You Look, the More You Find

Ascertainment Bias in Clinical Research and Practice

A teaching session for medical students

Opening Question

Two hospitals report postoperative blood clots (VTE):

Hospital A

5 VTEs per 1,000 surgical patients

Hospital B

13 VTEs per 1,000 surgical patients

Which hospital is safer?

Open with the provocative question and take a quick show of hands. Tell students they will be able to answer it by the end of the session. Most will choose Hospital A.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, you should be able to:

  1. Define ascertainment bias and explain how it distorts disease frequency and exposure–outcome associations
  2. Distinguish it from related biases (detection, surveillance, verification, screening-related biases, observer bias)
  3. Recognize it in classic and contemporary examples from epidemiology, genetics, quality measurement, infectious disease, and trials
  4. Predict the direction of the bias (over- vs. underestimation)
  5. Apply design and analysis strategies that prevent or reduce it

Warm-Up Case

In the 1970s, case-control studies reported that postmenopausal women taking estrogen had ~6 to 12 times the odds of endometrial cancer.

But estrogen causes uterine bleeding, and bleeding leads to D&C or biopsy.

Question: Were estrogen users really getting more cancer, or were doctors just finding more of the cancer that was already there?

Hold that thought; we come back to it in Example 1.

What Is Ascertainment Bias?

Ascertainment = the process by which study subjects, cases, or outcomes are identified.

Ascertainment bias = a systematic error that arises when the way cases or outcomes are identified differs between groups, or when the identified sample does not represent the population of interest.

Two main ways it shows up:

Mechanism What goes wrong Typical result
Who gets sampled Study population is enriched for people more likely to have (or be diagnosed with) the disease Inflated estimates of prevalence, penetrance, or severity
How outcomes are detected One group is examined, tested, or followed more intensively than another Spurious or exaggerated associations

Sources: Hamilton et al., Nature Reviews Clinical Oncology (2016), which describe ascertainment bias as a study population unrepresentative of the whole, usually at higher risk; the American Heart Association scientific statement on observational research (Mac Grory et al., 2026), which describes differential outcome detection driven by knowledge of exposure as a form of ascertainment bias.

A Note on Terminology

Textbooks don’t all use the same labels. Ascertainment bias often serves as an umbrella term, overlapping with:

Term Core idea
Detection bias Outcome detection differs between exposed and unexposed people
Surveillance bias Some groups are monitored more closely, so more outcomes are found
Diagnostic suspicion bias Knowing about an exposure prompts a more intensive search for disease
Verification (work-up) bias Only test-positive patients get the gold-standard test, which inflates sensitivity
Lead-time / length-time bias, overdiagnosis Screening finds disease earlier, and finds more slow-growing disease
Observer (assessor) bias Unblinded assessors judge outcomes differently by treatment arm

Some classify it as selection bias (sampling), others as information bias (measurement). The label matters less than asking how the cases were found.

Sources: Warden, “Definitions of Bias in Clinical Research,” Methods in Molecular Biology (2021); Mac Grory et al., AHA Scientific Statement (2026); Hamilton et al. (2016).

The Core Mechanism

Key insight: For many conditions there is a large hidden “reservoir” of undiagnosed disease, such as:

  • small thyroid cancers
  • asymptomatic DVTs
  • mild infections
  • subclinical prostate cancer

How hard you look decides how much of that reservoir you see.

Example 1: Estrogen and Endometrial Cancer

A classic debate

The bias hypothesis

Horwitz & Feinstein (NEJM, 1978):

  • Conventional case-control sampling: OR = 11.98
  • Alternative controls, all of whom had also undergone D&C or hysterectomy for uterine bleeding (that is, equally “looked at”): OR = 1.7
  • Their conclusion: detection bias had greatly overestimated the association

The rebuttal

  • Antunes et al. (NEJM, 1979; 451 cases): risk was ~6-fold overall and 15-fold with >5 years of use, and the data contradicted the “swifter diagnosis” explanation
  • Spengler et al. (1981): the association persisted after accounting for medical surveillance and prior D&C
  • Prospective cohorts later confirmed a real effect, e.g., the Nurses’ Health Study: RR 7.67 with ≥5 years of unopposed estrogen

Teaching point: Ascertainment bias was plausible and partly real, but it did not explain away a true causal effect. Trying to fix one bias can introduce another; Horwitz’s restricted controls were themselves criticized.

Emphasize that this debate shaped modern epidemiology. Unopposed estrogen does cause endometrial cancer, which is why progestogens are added for women with a uterus.

Example 2: Is Your Hospital “Bad” or Just Vigilant?

Postoperative VTE as a quality measure. Bilimoria et al., JAMA (2013), ~2,786 US hospitals:

Hospital imaging quartile VTE imaging studies / 1,000 discharges VTE diagnosed / 1,000 discharges
Lowest 32 5.0
Highest 167 13.5
  • Hospitals with higher structural quality scores had better prophylaxis adherence but higher risk-adjusted VTE rates (4.8 vs 6.4 per 1,000)
  • Prophylaxis adherence was positively correlated with VTE rates, the opposite of what was expected

Conclusion: measured VTE rates reflected how hard staff looked, not quality of care.

Supporting data. Veterans Affairs hospitals (Holcomb et al., JAMA Surgery, 2015): more inpatient imaging went with more inpatient VTE (R = 0.33), but not with fewer VTEs after discharge.

Real-world consequence: vigilant hospitals may be financially penalized in pay-for-performance programs (Yang & Bilimoria, JAMA, 2016).

Example 3: South Korea’s Thyroid Cancer “Epidemic”

  • In 1999, a national cancer screening program began. Thyroid ultrasound was not included, but hospitals offered it as a cheap add-on
  • By 2011, thyroid cancer diagnoses were 15× the 1993 rate, making it the most common cancer in the country (Ahn, Kim & Welch, NEJM, 2014)
  • Mortality stayed flat at ~300–400 deaths/year, while >40,000 people were diagnosed in 2011
  • 1999–2008: incidence rose 6.4-fold, and 94.4% of the increase was tumors <20 mm, detected mainly by screening (Park et al., BMJ, 2016)
  • About 90% of thyroid cancers in Korean women in 2008–2012 were attributed to overdiagnosis (Pizzato et al., Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology, 2022)

Why: autopsy studies show occult thyroid cancers in roughly 5–36% of people. Ultrasound simply reveals the reservoir.

Epilogue: public education campaigns cut thyroid surgeries by ~35% and incidence by ~30% (Chen & Haymart, Nature Reviews Endocrinology, 2026).

Rising incidence + stable mortality = the signature of ascertainment-driven overdiagnosis

Example 4: How Risky Is a BRCA Mutation?

Phenotype-first ascertainment. Early penetrance estimates came from families recruited because they had many cancers.

Study approach Breast cancer risk, BRCA1 Breast cancer risk, BRCA2
Retrospective family-based studies (to age 70) 40–87% 27–84%
Ashkenazi incident cases vs. population controls, no family selection (to age 70) 46% 26%
Meta-analysis adjusting for ascertainment (to age 70) 57% 49%
Large prospective cohort (to age 80) 72% 69%
  • Phenotype-first (clinic-referred) sampling substantially overestimates penetrance. Genotype-first (biobank) sampling can underestimate it because of recruitment biases (Wright et al., Nature Genetics, 2024)
  • Even population-based case probands inflate estimates if other risk factors cluster in families (Begg, JNCI, 2002)
  • The ACMG (2026) now frames inherited cancer risk as a continuum that depends on how the patient was ascertained

Clinical relevance: a variant found incidentally on population sequencing probably carries lower risk than the same variant found in a woman with a strong family history.

Sources: Kuchenbaecker et al., JAMA (2017); Satagopan et al., Cancer Epidemiology, Biomarkers & Prevention (2001); Chen & Parmigiani, Journal of Clinical Oncology (2007).

Example 5: How Deadly Was COVID-19?

Case fatality rate (CFR) = deaths ÷ confirmed cases

  • Early pandemic CFR: 17.3% across China, and >20% at the Wuhan epicenter (Quiroz et al., 2023)
  • Testing capacity was limited, so surveillance preferentially detected severe cases, which biased CFR upward (Verity et al., Lancet Infectious Diseases, 2020)
  • In Osaka, seroprevalence data showed true infections exceeded confirmed cases by up to 8.7-fold (ages 40–59) and 13.5-fold (≥60) during wave 2. Under-ascertainment rose again with mild Omicron infections (Zhang & Nishiura, Scientific Reports, 2023)

A competing bias

Deaths lag behind diagnoses (right-censoring), which biases early CFR downward.

Lesson: prefer the infection fatality rate (IFR), estimated with serosurveys or modeling, over the naive CFR.

Example 6: Everyday Clinical Data

PSA and benign prostatic hyperplasia. Men with lower urinary tract symptoms are more likely to have PSA checked, so more incidental prostate cancers are found, even though BPH has not been shown to cause cancer (Hamilton et al., 2016).

Anticoagulants and GI bleeding. Anticoagulated patients may undergo more endoscopy, so more bleeding events are recorded than in non-anticoagulated comparators (AHA Scientific Statement, 2026).

Migraine and type 2 diabetes. Patients with migraine have more visits and labs, so they have more chances to be diagnosed with diabetes, even with no true difference (Turner & Houle, Headache, 2019).

Drug safety monitoring. Drugs with mandated lab or skin checks generate more detected adverse events than drugs without monitoring requirements.

Example 7: Ascertainment Bias and Health Equity

  • Healthcare data (claims, EHRs) are not random samples of the population; they over-represent people with access to care
  • Groups facing barriers such as access, language, cost, or discrimination may be under-ascertained. Their disease looks less common than it truly is, or is diagnosed later and more severely
  • Example: apparent racial and ethnic differences in atrial fibrillation incidence in real-world data may partly reflect differential ascertainment rather than biology (Hulstaert et al., PLoS One, 2023)

Takeaway

Before concluding “Disease X is rarer in Group Y,” ask whether Group Y is tested as often.

Screening: A Special Case

When screen-detected patients are compared with clinically detected patients, three ascertainment-related biases make screening look better than it is:

Bias What happens Effect
Lead-time bias Diagnosis is moved earlier; death date is unchanged Survival from diagnosis looks longer
Length-time bias Periodic screening preferentially catches slow-growing tumors Screen-detected cancers have a better prognosis
Overdiagnosis Detects cancers that would never have caused symptoms Inflated incidence and “cure” rates
  • About 50% (range 20–72%) of the survival advantage of screen-detected breast cancers may come from lead-time and length bias alone (Shwartz, Cancer, 1980)
  • The proportion of localized cancers always rises with screening, whether or not screening works (Keen & Jørgensen, Journal of Women’s Health, 2015)

Rule

Judge screening by mortality reduction in RCTs, not by survival from diagnosis (Frame, JAMA, 2000; Midthun & Jett, 2013).

Ascertainment Bias in Randomized Trials

Randomization balances groups at baseline, but outcome ascertainment can still be biased when assessors know the treatment assignment.

  • Meta-epidemiological study of 66 trials: non-blinded outcome assessors exaggerated odds ratios by 29% on average (95% CI 8–45%) (Salazar et al., Journal of Clinical Epidemiology, 2025)
  • Systematic survey of 41 meta-epidemiological studies: lack of assessor blinding overestimates effects for subjective outcomes (ROR 0.69; high certainty) (Wang et al., 2024)
  • Counterpoint: the large MetaBLIND study found no clear average difference (Moustgaard et al., BMJ, 2020). The impact likely varies by outcome and context

Practical fix: even when surgeons and patients can’t be blinded, outcome assessors usually can be, for example by an external reviewer rating photographs of wounds (CONSORT 2025).

Hard outcomes such as all-cause death are largely immune; soft outcomes such as pain, “improvement,” and radiographic progression are vulnerable.

Which Way Does the Bias Push?

Scenario Usual direction
Exposed group examined more intensively Overestimates association (OR/RR biased away from 1)
Unexposed group examined more intensively Underestimates or can reverse the association
Sample enriched for severe or familial cases Overestimates severity, penetrance, CFR
Sample drawn from people with poor access to testing Underestimates prevalence
Equal but imperfect detection in all groups (nondifferential) Usually biases toward the null (with exceptions)

Source: AHA Scientific Statement on Observational Research (Mac Grory et al., 2026).

How to Spot It When Reading a Paper

  1. How were cases identified? Clinic referrals? Registries? Screening? Biobank?
  2. Were all groups examined with equal intensity (same tests, same visit frequency, same follow-up duration)?
  3. Did the exposure itself cause symptoms or contact with care that would prompt testing (e.g., bleeding, side effects, monitoring requirements)?
  4. Were outcome assessors blinded to exposure or treatment?
  5. Is the outcome subjective or prone to subclinical detection?
  6. Does incidence rise while mortality stays flat?
  7. Would the result change in a population-based, genotype-first, or serosurvey sample?

Prevention and Mitigation

Stage Strategy Example
Design Population-based or random sampling Population cohorts and biobanks for penetrance
Design Standardized, protocol-driven outcome ascertainment for everyone Same imaging schedule in all arms
Design Blind outcome assessors Central adjudication committees; photo-based wound assessment
Design Choose comparison groups with equal diagnostic scrutiny Horwitz–Feinstein “equally examined” controls
Design Use hard or objective endpoints All-cause mortality rather than survival from diagnosis
Analysis Adjust for healthcare use or follow-up intensity Number of visits/tests as a covariate
Analysis Use outcomes robust to follow-up variability Sustained disability rather than single-visit relapse
Analysis Estimate true denominators Serosurveys for IFR; statistical correction for ascertainment
Analysis Sensitivity analyses / negative control outcomes Check whether “unrelated” diagnoses are also inflated

Sources: AHA Scientific Statement (2026); CONSORT 2025 Explanation and Elaboration (Hopewell et al.); Kalincik et al., Brain (2023); Wright et al., Nature Genetics (2024).

Practice Vignettes (1/2)

Vignette A. A cohort study finds that patients starting a new immunosuppressant have 2× the rate of diagnosed skin cancer. The drug label requires dermatology exams every 3 months; comparators have no such requirement.

Answer: Detection/surveillance bias. The exposed group is examined far more often. Fixes: compare with an active comparator that has similar monitoring, restrict to invasive or advanced cancers, or adjust for dermatology visits.

Vignette B. A cardiogenetics clinic reports 80% penetrance for a cardiomyopathy variant in referred families. A national biobank estimates 15%.

Answer: Phenotype-first ascertainment bias in the clinic estimate (families came because they were affected). The biobank estimate is likely closer to the truth for incidental carriers, though it may slightly underestimate because of healthy-volunteer recruitment.

Practice Vignettes (2/2)

Vignette C. Five-year survival is 80% for screen-detected lung cancers versus 15% for symptom-detected cancers. A newspaper headline says “Screening saves lives!”

Answer: Lead-time bias, length-time bias, and overdiagnosis can all produce this survival gap even if screening doesn’t work. Mortality reduction in a randomized trial is required.

Vignette D. In an open-label surgical trial, the operating surgeons grade wound healing at 30 days. The new technique looks markedly better.

Answer: Observer/assessor ascertainment bias with a subjective outcome. Fix: blinded independent assessors, e.g., standardized wound photographs graded centrally.

Key Takeaways

  1. Ascertainment bias = differences in how cases or outcomes are found, not in how often they truly occur
  2. “The more you look, the more you find,” especially for diseases with a large subclinical reservoir
  3. It can create, inflate, mask, or even reverse associations
  4. Classic examples: estrogen–endometrial cancer, VTE quality metrics, Korean thyroid cancer, BRCA penetrance, early COVID-19 CFR
  5. Watch for rising incidence with flat mortality, unequal testing intensity, referral-based samples, and unblinded soft outcomes
  6. Best defenses: population-based sampling, standardized ascertainment, blinded assessment, hard endpoints, and RCT mortality data for screening

Always ask: “How did these patients, or these outcomes, get counted?”

Return to the opening question. The hospital reporting 13 VTEs per 1,000 may well be the safer, more vigilant one.
